## 13 – Job Market for Problem Definition Skills

This file explains how **Problem Definition skills** appear in real job roles and career paths.

We cover:

- A. Roles that depend heavily on Problem Definition
- B. How job descriptions talk about these skills
- C. Career progression

---

### A. Roles That Depend Heavily on Problem Definition

Even if the title doesn’t say “Problem Definition”, many jobs rely on it.

#### 1. Product Manager / Senior Product Manager

Typical responsibilities in job ads:

- “Define and communicate clear problem statements and requirements.”
- “Translate business goals and customer needs into actionable problems.”
- “Work with stakeholders to align on what should be solved.”

Core discovery + framing skills are essential.

---

#### 2. Business Analyst / Systems Analyst

Responsibilities:

- Understand business processes and pain points.
- Document as‑is and to‑be states.
- Clarify requirements with stakeholders.

These roles often do **Problem Definition in a more operational or IT‑focused way**.

---

#### 3. Strategy & Consulting Roles

Titles:

- Strategy Consultant, Management Consultant, Strategy Manager.

Responsibilities:

- Analyze client or business problems,
- frame them in structured ways,
- propose high‑level solution options.

Problem framing is **central** to their work.

---

#### 4. UX / Service Designers

Responsibilities:

- Map journeys,
- uncover and synthesize user problems,
- collaborate on problem statements used by product and engineering.

They may not always “own” the final statement, but they provide **critical input**.

---

### B. How Job Descriptions Mention Problem Definition

Look for phrases like:

- “Define and prioritize problems and opportunities.”
- “Clarify problem statements and success metrics.”
- “Translate stakeholder needs into well‑formed problems and requirements.”
- “Work with cross‑functional teams to align on the problem space.”

In banking/enterprise contexts:

- “Ensure problem statements account for regulatory, risk, and operational constraints.”
- “Collaborate with risk, compliance, and operations to define feasible problem scopes.”

If a role emphasizes:

- “requirements gathering”,
- “business needs analysis”,
- “root cause analysis”,

it likely needs strong Problem Definition capability.

---

### C. Career Progression & Impact

#### 1. From Junior PM/Analyst to Senior PM

Early career:

- You might be given:
  - pre‑defined tasks or features,
  - with limited input into **what** should be solved.

As you grow:

- You are expected to:
  - **challenge and refine** problem statements,
  - sometimes say:
    - “This is not the right problem to solve now.”

Demonstrating:

- strong Problem Definition work (with evidence and alignment),

helps you move into roles with:

- more ownership and influence.

---

#### 2. From Senior PM to Product Lead / Director

At higher levels, you:

- oversee **multiple problem spaces**,
- help teams:
  - pick the **right problems** from a portfolio of opportunities.

Problem Definition at this level looks like:

- facilitating **cross‑team alignment** on:
  - shared definitions,
  - shared metrics.

Strong performance here is a key factor for:

- promotions to Product Lead / Group PM / Director roles.

---

#### 3. Moves into Strategy, Consulting, or Ops Leadership

Because Problem Definition sits at the intersection of:

- strategy,
- process,
- customer,
- technology,

it is an excellent foundation for:

- strategy or corporate development roles,
- consulting positions,
- operations leadership positions in complex organizations.

---

### D. How to Demonstrate These Skills in CVs and Interviews

Instead of only listing features delivered, highlight:

- **Problem clarity**
  - “Defined the core problem as high SME onboarding abandonment at document upload step, affecting 600 cases/month.”
- **Evidence**
  - “Used funnel analysis + 12 customer interviews + 200 support tickets.”
- **Alignment**
  - “Aligned business, risk, and ops on scope and metrics in a 3‑week Problem Definition sprint.”
- **Impact**
  - “Led to redesign that later reduced abandonment by 20 percentage points.”

This shows:

- structured thinking,
- cross‑functional collaboration,
- outcome orientation.
