## 05 – Methods Overview (User & Market Research)

This file summarizes **common research methods**, when to use them, and what they’re good for.  
It is meant as a **menu** you can combine based on your goals.

We group methods into:

- A. Qualitative user research
- B. Quantitative research
- C. Market & competitive research

---

### A. Qualitative User Research Methods

#### 1. Semi‑Structured Interviews

**What it is**

- 1:1 conversations guided by a discussion outline but open to follow‑ups.

**Use when**

- you need to:
  - understand goals, behaviors, and context,
  - explore how users experience a problem,
  - get rich quotes and stories.

**Strengths**

- Deep insight into **why** and **how**.
- Flexible: you can dig deeper into unexpected areas.

**Limitations**

- Small samples → not statistically representative.
- Requires good moderation to avoid leading questions.

---

#### 2. Contextual Inquiry / Shadowing

**What it is**

- Observing users in their real environment while they perform tasks, sometimes asking questions as they go.

**Use when**

- workflows are:
  - complex,
  - multi‑tool,
  - heavily influenced by local practices (e.g., branch staff, back‑office operations).

**Strengths**

- Reveals:
  - unofficial workarounds,
  - true process flows,
  - environmental constraints.

**Limitations**

- Time‑consuming,
- requires access and trust.

---

#### 3. Usability Testing

**What it is**

- Asking users to perform tasks using:
  - existing products,
  - prototypes,
while you observe and collect metrics (e.g., task success, errors, time).

**Use when**

- evaluating how well **current or proposed designs** support tasks.

**Strengths**

- Very good at catching:
  - UX issues,
  - comprehension gaps,
  - friction in flows.

**Limitations**

- Focuses more on **interaction quality** than broader context or motivations.

---

#### 4. Workshops (e.g., Journey Mapping)

**What it is**

- Group sessions with users and/or internal stakeholders to:
  - map journeys,
  - surface pain points,
  - co‑create ideas.

**Use when**

- you need to:
  - consolidate knowledge,
  - align multiple perspectives,
  - quickly generate a shared view.

**Strengths**

- Efficient for synthesis,
- builds shared understanding.

**Limitations**

- Group dynamics and loud voices can skew outputs,
- requires facilitation skills.

---

#### 5. Diary Studies (Longitudinal)

**What it is**

- Participants record activities, experiences, or feelings over time (e.g., daily logs).

**Use when**

- behaviors unfold over days/weeks:
  - cash‑flow management,
  - recurring tasks,
  - multi‑step journeys.

**Strengths**

- Captures:
  - patterns and variability over time.

**Limitations**

- Higher participant burden,
- risk of incomplete logs.

---

### B. Quantitative Research Methods

#### 1. Product Analytics / Telemetry

**What it is**

- Instrumented events and logs showing how users move through products.

**Use when**

- measuring:
  - funnels,
  - retention,
  - feature usage,
  - behavioral segments.

**Strengths**

- Shows **what actually happens at scale**.

**Limitations**

- Doesn’t explain **why** users behave that way.
- Requires good event design and data quality.

---

#### 2. Surveys

**What they are**

- Structured questionnaires distributed to larger samples (online, in‑product, email).

**Use when**

- you need:
  - numeric estimates of attitudes or preferences,
  - to validate patterns seen in qualitative work,
  - to segment users by self‑reported traits.

**Strengths**

- Efficient for large samples,
- can be repeated over time.

**Limitations**

- Sensitive to:
  - question wording,
  - sampling bias,
  - response bias.

---

#### 3. A/B Tests & Experiments (Later Phases)

**What they are**

- Controlled experiments where different users see different variants.

**Use when**

- you have:
  - design or content alternatives,
  - and want to see which perform better on target metrics.

**Note**

- Usually more aligned to **solution validation** than early problem/needs discovery,
- but can complement research where hypotheses are specific.

---

### C. Market & Competitive Research Methods

#### 1. Competitive Product Reviews

**What it is**

- Systematic evaluation of competitor products:
  - features,
  - flows,
  - UX,
  - messaging and positioning.

**Use when**

- you need to:
  - understand table‑stakes and differentiators,
  - see how competitors solve similar problems.

**Strengths**

- Helps avoid:
  - reinventing basic patterns,
  - missing obvious gaps.

**Limitations**

- You see only the **tip of the iceberg** (public surface, not internal strategy).

---

#### 2. Desk Research & Industry Reports

**What it is**

- Reviewing:
  - analyst reports,
  - industry studies,
  - academic and practitioner articles.

**Use when**

- you need:
  - macro context,
  - benchmarks,
  - trend overviews.

**Strengths**

- Quick access to **high‑level insights**.

**Limitations**

- May not directly answer your specific product questions,
- can be outdated or biased.

---

#### 3. Expert & Stakeholder Interviews

**What they are**

- Conversations with:
  - internal experts (ops, risk, sales),
  - external experts (consultants, industry veterans).

**Use when**

- you need:
  - domain context,
  - current practices,
  - emerging trends from practitioner perspective.

**Strengths**

- Efficient knowledge acquisition.

**Limitations**

- Subject to expert biases,
- must be balanced with user‑facing research.

---

### How to Choose Methods

Use this quick mapping:

- Need to understand **behaviors & context** → interviews, contextual inquiry, shadowing.
- Need to measure **scale/frequency** → analytics, surveys.
- Need to evaluate **flows or designs** → usability tests, A/B tests (later).
- Need to know **market table‑stakes/differentiators** → competitive reviews, desk research.

Often, a strong research plan combines:

- 1–2 qualitative methods,
- 1–2 quantitative analyses,
- 1 market/competitive scan.
