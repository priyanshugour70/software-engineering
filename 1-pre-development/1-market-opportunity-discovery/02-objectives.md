## 02 – Objectives of Market Opportunity Discovery

This file explains **what success looks like** in the Market Opportunity Discovery phase.  
Use it as a **north star** when running activities or reviewing outputs.


### 1. Primary Objective (One-Sentence Summary)

The main objective is to **build a well‑prioritized portfolio of clearly articulated opportunity hypotheses** that:

- align with **strategy**,
- are supported by **evidence and signals**, and
- are ready for **deeper validation** in later phases (research, business case, feasibility).


### 2. Key Outcomes You Should Achieve

#### 2.1 Clear, Written Opportunity Definitions

For each top opportunity, you should have a **short, structured description**, often called an **opportunity brief** (see templates later):

- **Who** (target segment / customer type)
- **What** (problem/pain/need)
- **Why it matters** (impact and urgency for them)
- **Why now** (market / regulatory / competitive timing)
- **Why us** (fit with our capabilities and strategy)
- **How big it could be** (rough magnitude – qualitative or quantitative)
- **Main assumptions and unknowns**

If you cannot explain the opportunity in **1–2 pages with this structure**, it is not yet ready to move forward.


#### 2.2 Prioritized Opportunity Backlog

You should not only have a **list** of opportunities, but also a **prioritized view** based on:

- **Strategic alignment** – does it contribute to key company or product OKRs?
- **Value potential** – is the upside meaningful for your business?
- **Customer/user impact** – does it solve painful or important problems?
- **Feasibility (rough)** – does it seem technically and operationally doable?
- **Risk and uncertainty** – how many unknowns and how severe are they?

Outcome: 

- A **ranked backlog** or **opportunity radar** that helps leadership and teams decide **where to focus next**.


#### 2.3 Shared Understanding Across Stakeholders

Different functions (product, business, engineering, UX, compliance, sales) should share:

- A **common language** for the top opportunities.
- A **shared view of the problem space**, not just individual opinions.
- Awareness of **key assumptions and risks**.

Success signs:

- Stakeholders can **describe the top 3–5 opportunities in similar ways**.
- Discussions shift from “I like this idea” to “This opportunity is strong because…”.


#### 2.4 Early Risk and Constraint Awareness

Even though detailed feasibility comes later, discovery should surface:

- **Regulatory constraints** (licensing, reporting, KYC/AML, data residency).
- **Technical constraints** (core system limits, integrations, data quality).
- **Operational constraints** (people, processes, support models).

Objectives here:

- Avoid pushing **completely unrealistic** opportunities downstream.
- Flag where **deep feasibility or risk assessment** will be needed later.


#### 2.5 Evidence‑Linked Opportunities

Each opportunity should be connected to **real signals**, not just opinions:

- Customer complaints, NPS comments, interviews.
- Product usage data, funnel metrics, revenue data.
- Industry reports, analyst notes, competitor moves.
- Regulatory documents or central bank communications.

Objective:

- For each opportunity, you can answer:  
  **“What data or observations led us to believe this is important?”**


### 3. Secondary (But Important) Objectives

#### 3.1 Build a Habit of Continuous Discovery

Objective:

- Create a **repeatable, ongoing practice** rather than a one‑off project.

Signs of success:

- Regular **opportunity reviews** (e.g., quarterly).
- A living **opportunity backlog** that is updated with new signals.
- Clear ownership of the discovery process.


#### 3.2 Improve Quality of Strategic Conversations

When done well, opportunity discovery:

- Moves leadership conversations from **feature lists** to **opportunity portfolios**.
- Makes trade‑offs explicit: “If we invest in Opportunity A, we are not investing in B.”
- Connects **product decisions** to **business strategy and metrics**.


#### 3.3 Educate the Organization About the Market

By gathering and structuring signals, you:

- Help teams **understand customers and the market** more deeply.
- Spread awareness of **trends, threats, and new spaces**.
- Reduce reliance on **anecdotes** and “HIPPO” (Highest Paid Person’s Opinion).


### 4. What Good vs Bad Output Looks Like

#### 4.1 “Good” Output

- A **short document or page** per opportunity that:
  - clearly describes the **customer and problem**,
  - explains **why now and why us**,
  - includes **references to data or observations**,
  - lists **assumptions and risks**.
- A **prioritized view** (top 3–10 opportunities) with clear reasoning.
- Stakeholders **can explain and defend the ranking**.


#### 4.2 “Bad” Output

- A messy, unstructured **idea list** like:
  - “Build AI chatbot”
  - “Add crypto support”
  - “Do something with open banking”
- No connection to:
  - strategy,
  - quantified value,
  - customer problems,
  - evidence.
- Stakeholders disagree on:
  - what the opportunity even is,
  - which opportunities matter most.


### 5. Example: Objectives for a Banking Discovery Cycle

Imagine a bank runs a 6‑week Market Opportunity Discovery initiative for **SME segment digitization**.

They set specific objectives such as:

1. **Map the landscape**
   - Identify at least **15–20 potential opportunity areas** across lending, payments, treasury, and advisory for SMEs.
2. **Produce structured briefs**
   - Create **short opportunity briefs** for at least **10 opportunities** using a consistent template.
3. **Prioritize**
   - Produce a **ranked list of top 5 opportunities** with reasoning based on:
     - strategic fit,
     - potential value,
     - feasibility level,
     - urgency.
4. **Prepare for next phases**
   - For the top 3 opportunities, define:
     - **key assumptions** to validate,
     - **data or research** needed next,
     - **stakeholders to involve** in Problem Definition.

These objectives keep the team **focused** and give leadership **clear expectations**.


### 6. Checklist: Have We Met the Objectives?

Use this quick checklist at the end of the discovery cycle:

- **Clarity**
  - [ ] Can we explain each top opportunity in 1–2 pages?
  - [ ] Do we clearly state **who**, **what problem**, and **why now**?
- **Alignment**
  - [ ] Does each top opportunity link to at least one **strategic objective or OKR**?
- **Evidence**
  - [ ] Do we reference specific **data, observations, or documents** for each?
- **Prioritization**
  - [ ] Do we have a **ranked list** (not just a flat list)?
  - [ ] Is the ranking based on **explicit criteria**?
- **Next Steps**
  - [ ] For each top opportunity, do we know **what to validate next**?
  - [ ] Are **owners** identified for the next phase?

If you cannot tick most of these boxes, go back and **refine the outputs** before handing them off to downstream phases.
