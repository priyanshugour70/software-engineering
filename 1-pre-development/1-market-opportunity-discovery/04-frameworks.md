## 04 – Frameworks for Market Opportunity Discovery

Frameworks are **thinking tools** that help you structure and communicate your analysis.  
This file introduces practical frameworks you can actually use in discovery:

- A. **Jobs‑To‑Be‑Done (JTBD)**
- B. **3C (Customer, Company, Competitor)**
- C. **Porter’s Five Forces (lightweight)**
- D. **Problem / Solution / Outcome framing**
- E. **Opportunity Solution Tree (OST)**
- F. **Market Map / Landscape**

You do not need to use all of them at once. Start with **1–2 frameworks**, then add more as needed.

---

### A. Jobs‑To‑Be‑Done (JTBD)

**Idea**: Customers “hire” a product to get a **job** done in their life or business.

In discovery, JTBD helps you:

- Focus on **outcomes and progress** instead of features.
- Identify **alternative solutions** customers use today (competitors or workarounds).

**Basic JTBD Statement**

> “When **[situation]**, I want to **[motivation / job]**, so I can **[expected outcome]**.”

**Example (Banking – SME Lending)**

- When: “cash flow is tight at the end of the month and supplier invoices are due,”  
- I want to: “quickly access a short‑term credit line without complex paperwork,”  
- So I can: “pay my suppliers on time and avoid damaging relationships.”

**Usage in Discovery**

- During interviews, ask:
  - “What were you trying to get done?”
  - “How did you try to do it?”
  - “What made it hard or frustrating?”
- Cluster JTBD statements to reveal **recurring jobs** worth deeper exploration.

### B. 3C Framework (Customer, Company, Competitor)

The **3C** framework helps you consider opportunities from three angles:

1. **Customer** – what do customers need and value?
2. **Company** – what can we uniquely provide?
3. **Competitor** – what are others offering and how are they positioned?

**Simple 3C Table**

| C         | Key Questions                                                                    |
|----------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Customer | Who are they? What are their key jobs & pains? What do they value most?          |
| Company  | What assets and capabilities do we have? What constraints?                       |
| Competitor | Who else serves this need? Where are they strong/weak? What’s missing today?  |

**Example (Digital SME Credit Line)**

- **Customer**
  - Want faster, transparent credit decisions.
  - Hate paperwork and branch visits.
  - Need flexible limits aligned with cash‑flow cycles.
- **Company**
  - Strong risk models and deep SME transaction data.
  - Branch network but weak digital flows today.
  - Regulatory licenses already in place.
- **Competitor**
  - Fintech lenders with fast onboarding but shallow data.
  - Other banks piloting simple online flows.

**Usage in Discovery**

- Use 3C during **synthesis and shaping** (see `03-workflow.md`) to:
  - Check if an opportunity is a **good strategic fit**.
  - Understand **where you can differentiate**.

### C. Porter’s Five Forces (Lightweight Version)

Porter’s Five Forces help you understand **industry attractiveness**.  
For discovery, use a **lightweight version** to avoid getting stuck in academic detail.

The five forces are:

1. **Threat of new entrants**
2. **Threat of substitutes**
3. **Bargaining power of buyers**
4. **Bargaining power of suppliers**
5. **Rivalry among existing competitors**

**How to Apply Quickly**

Pick an opportunity and ask 1–2 questions per force:

- **New entrants** – Is it easy for startups or tech giants to enter this space?
- **Substitutes** – Can customers solve this need in a completely different way?
- **Buyer power** – Do customers have many options and strong negotiation power?
- **Supplier power** – Are there key data, infrastructure, or partners that can dictate terms?
- **Rivalry** – Is the space already crowded, with heavy price competition?

**Example (Cross‑Border SMB Payments)**

- New entrants: Many fintechs, low barrier with modern rails.
- Substitutes: Traditional bank transfers, FX brokers, card rails.
- Buyer power: SMBs have choices; they can switch providers relatively easily.
- Supplier power: Dependence on correspondent banks / FX liquidity providers.
- Rivalry: Increasing – big banks, fintechs, and global platforms all competing.

**Usage**

- Use this framework to **sense check** if an opportunity space is:
  - attractive long‑term,
  - or likely to suffer from **thin margins and heavy competition**.

### D. Problem / Solution / Outcome Framing

This simple framework helps you keep focus on the **right sequence**:

1. **Problem (Opportunity)**
   - Who is affected?
   - What is their situation and pain?
   - Why is it important now?
2. **Solution (Later)**
   - How might we address this?
3. **Outcome (For customer and business)**
   - What changes for them?
   - What changes for us (metrics, revenue, risk, etc.)?

In Market Opportunity Discovery, we mostly stay on the **Problem and Outcome** levels.

**Example**

- **Problem**:  
  “SME owners cannot easily forecast short‑term cash‑flow, causing stress and sub‑optimal decisions.”
- **Outcome**:  
  “SMEs gain confidence and can make timely decisions; bank sees more product usage and lower default risk.”
- **Solution** (later phases):  
  “Cash‑flow forecasting dashboard integrated into their account and payment data.”

Usage:

- Structure opportunity briefs and discussions using P/S/O headings.
- When conversations drift to features, ask:  
  “Which **problem** and **outcome** is this feature serving?”

### E. Opportunity Solution Tree (OST)

An **Opportunity Solution Tree** (popularized by Teresa Torres) visually connects:

- **Outcome** (top)
- **Opportunities** (middle)
- **Solutions/Experiments** (bottom)

In the discovery phase, you mostly work on the **Outcome and Opportunity levels**.

**Basic Structure**

- **Outcome**: “Increase SME digital engagement by 20% in 12 months.”
  - **Opportunity 1**: SMEs struggle with visibility into cash‑flow.
  - **Opportunity 2**: SMEs find onboarding and documentation too slow.
  - **Opportunity 3**: SMEs lack proactive alerts for important events.

Later, each opportunity can link to:

- Experiments,
- Features,
- Product ideas.

Usage:

- OST helps visualize **how multiple opportunities relate to a bigger outcome**.
- Useful in portfolio and roadmap discussions.

### F. Market Map / Landscape

A **market map** is a visual representation of:

- Segments (e.g., micro‑SMEs vs SMEs vs large corporates),
- Use cases (e.g., lending, payments, treasury, analytics),
- Player types (banks, fintechs, platforms, infra providers).

**Simple Market Map Example (for SME Financial Services)**

Columns: **Use Cases** (Accounts, Lending, Payments, Analytics)  
Rows: **Player Types** (Traditional Banks, Challenger Banks, Fintechs, SaaS Platforms)

In each cell, list major players and approximate positioning.

Usage:

- Identify **white spaces** where:
  - few players operate,
  - or current offerings are weak for certain segments.
- Identify **over‑served** areas where margins may be thin.

---

### How to Choose Frameworks (Beginner Guidance)

Start with:

- **JTBD** for understanding what customers are trying to achieve.
- **Problem / Outcome framing** to keep focus on opportunity space.
- **3C** to align with company strategy and competitive reality.

Then add:

- **OST** when connecting opportunities to product outcomes and roadmaps.
- **Five Forces** and **Market Maps** when evaluating **industry attractiveness** or justifying big strategic bets.
