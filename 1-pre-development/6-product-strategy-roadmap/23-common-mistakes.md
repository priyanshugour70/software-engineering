## 23 – Common Mistakes in Product Strategy & Roadmapping

This file lists **frequent mistakes** teams make in Product Strategy & Roadmap work, especially in enterprises and banks, and suggests how to avoid them.

Use it as an **anti‑pattern catalog**.

---

### 1. Confusing Roadmaps with Detailed Project Plans

Symptoms:

- roadmaps:
  - have exact dates for 12–24 months ahead,
  - list dozens of features per quarter,
  - rarely change despite new information.

Problems:

- creates **fake certainty**,
- stakeholders assume:
  - everything is committed,
  - changes are failures rather than learning.

Better approach:

- use multi‑horizon roadmaps:
  - detailed near term, fuzzy later (see `07-roadmap-levels-and-views.md`).
- focus:
  - on outcomes and themes,
  - not long lists of features with artificial deadlines.

---

### 2. Strategy as a One‑Off Document Nobody Uses

Symptoms:

- 40+ slide “strategy deck” created once a year,
- then:
  - stored in a folder,
  - rarely referenced in day‑to‑day decisions.

Problems:

- teams:
  - lose sight of direction and trade‑offs,
  - revert to:
    - feature requests,
    - stakeholder lobbying.

Better approach:

- keep strategy:
  - short, living document (3–5 pages) (see `04-vision-and-product-strategy.md`).
- reference it:
  - in roadmap reviews and prioritization sessions,
  - when evaluating new ideas or requests.

---

### 3. No Clear Themes or Outcome Goals

Symptoms:

- backlog and roadmap:
  - are long lists of unrelated items,
  - without clear grouping or focus.

Problems:

- hard to:
  - understand what you are trying to achieve,
  - allocate capacity meaningfully,
  - explain why something is prioritized.

Better approach:

- define:
  - 3–7 strategic themes,
  - with objectives and metrics (see `05-strategic-themes-goals-and-okrs.md`).
- tag:
  - initiatives and epics by theme,
  - visualize them in roadmap views.

---

### 4. Ignoring Capacity and Over‑Committing

Symptoms:

- roadmap:
  - seems to promise more work than teams can realistically deliver,
  - everything is “top priority.”

Problems:

- frequent:
  - missed dates,
  - frustrated teams,
  - rushed quality,
  - poor trust with stakeholders.

Better approach:

- base roadmap:
  - on real capacity estimates (see `09-capacity-planning-and-funding-models.md`).
- be clear:
  - about what fits in the horizon,
  - what moves to later or is not included.

---

### 5. Under‑Representing Platform, Risk, and Ops Work

Symptoms:

- roadmap:
  - dominated by user‑visible features,
  - little explicit platform, risk, or operations work.

Problems:

- technical debt and risk accumulate,
- outages, compliance issues, and manual processes grow,
- teams resort to hidden work not visible in strategy.

Better approach:

- allocate explicit capacity for:
  - platform,
  - risk & compliance,
  - ops improvements.
- make:
  - these initiatives visible in roadmaps and themes.

---

### 6. Treating Risk & Compliance as Gatekeepers Only

Symptoms:

- risk and compliance:
  - only see proposals at the last stage,
  - mainly say “no” or require big re‑work.

Problems:

- delays,
- frustration,
- adversarial relationships,
- lost opportunities for innovative but safe designs.

Better approach:

- involve:
  - risk/compliance early in strategy and roadmap discussions.
- co‑design:
  - guardrails and controls,
  - feasible sequences of change.

---

### 7. Not Managing Dependencies Explicitly

Symptoms:

- cross‑team dependencies:
  - only tracked informally,
  - discovered late.

Problems:

- blocked teams,
- missed deadlines,
- surprise integration work.

Better approach:

- identify dependencies:
  - during initiative and epic shaping (see `10-dependencies-and-sequencing.md`).
- visualize them:
  - program boards,
  - diagrams,
  - issue links in tools.

---

### 8. Constantly Changing Frameworks and Processes

Symptoms:

- each quarter:
  - a new prioritization method,
  - a new roadmap template,
  - a new governance forum.

Problems:

- teams:
  - never build intuition,
  - suffer from process fatigue,
  - treat frameworks as fads.

Better approach:

- pick:
  - a **simple** set of frameworks (e.g., RICE or WSJF + themes + multi‑horizon roadmaps).
- stick with them:
  - long enough for teams to learn and adapt,
  - only refine when clearly needed.

---

### 9. Over‑Indexing on Tools Instead of Practices

Symptoms:

- long tool evaluation and configuration,
- belief that:
  - “buying X roadmap tool will fix our strategy and alignment.”

Problems:

- tools:
  - encode existing bad habits,
  - create overhead without improving thinking.

Better approach:

- focus on:
  - clarity of vision, themes, and outcomes,
  - recurring alignment cadences,
  - visible, living roadmaps.
- let tools:
  - support good practices,
  - not replace them.

---

### 10. Not Communicating Changes Clearly

Symptoms:

- roadmap changes:
  - quietly in tools,
  - but stakeholders are surprised later.

Problems:

- loss of trust,
- perception of “random changes” or chaos.

Better approach:

- when changing roadmaps:
  - communicate:
    - what changed,
    - why it changed (new info, constraints, learnings),
    - impact on expectations.
- use:
  - review forums,
  - short update notes,
  - updated visuals.

---

### 11. Beginner Checklist

- [ ] Do we:
  - [ ] have clear themes and outcome goals?
  - [ ] align roadmaps with capacity?
  - [ ] make platform/risk/ops work visible?
  - [ ] involve risk/compliance and ops early?
  - [ ] manage dependencies explicitly?
  - [ ] communicate roadmap changes with rationale?

If several answers are “no,” pick the most painful anti‑pattern from this file and start addressing it deliberately.
