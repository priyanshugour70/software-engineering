## 04 – Vision & Product Strategy (How to Craft Them)

This file helps you **write and refine**:

- a clear **product vision**, and
- a practical, living **product strategy**.

It assumes you already know the basics from `01-concepts.md` and the objectives from `02-objectives.md`.

---

### 1. Product Vision – A Simple, Strong Definition

**Product vision** describes:

- **who** you serve,
- **what** you enable them to do,
- **why** it matters (value),
- and sometimes **how** you’ll be different.

It should be:

- short (1–3 sentences),
- understandable to **non‑experts**,
- inspiring but **not marketing fluff**,
- stable for several years (does not change every quarter).

#### 1.1 Vision Formula (Beginner Friendly)

A simple template:

> “For \<who>, we provide \<what>, so they can \<value>, in a way that is \<differentiator>.”

Example (digital SME lending):

> “For small and medium businesses, we provide fast, transparent access to tailored credit, so they can grow with confidence, in a way that combines digital convenience with responsible, data‑driven risk management.”

You can tweak wording, but keep the **core elements**.

---

### 2. From Vision to Strategy

If vision is **“where we want to go”**, then strategy is:

> “Given our starting point and constraints, **how** will we move toward that vision, and **what will we not do**?”

Product strategy:

- sits **between** company/segment strategy and team‑level roadmaps,
- turns broad ambition into **a set of choices**:
  - which problems and segments,
  - which value props,
  - which capabilities and platforms,
  - which partnership or ecosystem plays,
  - which risks and technical constraints we accept or must reduce.

---

### 3. Components of a Good Product Strategy

Think of strategy as **a concise document + a shared mental model**, not a huge slide deck.

Key sections often include:

1. **Context & Diagnosis**
2. **Vision (Recap)**
3. **Strategic Pillars / Themes**
4. **Where We Will Play / Not Play**
5. **How We Will Win / Be Valuable**
6. **Key Bets & Capabilities**
7. **Risks, Constraints & Assumptions**

#### 3.1 Context & Diagnosis

Answer:

- What is happening in:
  - the market,
  - our customers’ world,
  - our internal organization and tech?
- What is our **honest starting point**?

Examples:

- “Our SME lending experience is slow and opaque compared to digital competitors.”
- “Our risk models are strong but hard to change; this slows new product launches.”

Keep this section:

- **evidence‑based** (data + insights),
- **short** (1–2 pages max).

#### 3.2 Strategic Pillars / Themes

Translate vision and diagnosis into **3–7 pillars**, for example:

- “Frictionless Digital Experience”
- “Configurable Risk & Compliance Engine”
- “Operational Excellence & Automation”

Each pillar should have:

- intent (why this matters),
- early thinking on capabilities and metrics,
- examples of initiatives that belong under it.

#### 3.3 Where We Will Play / Not Play

Be explicit about **scope boundaries**:

- which customer segments,
- which geographies,
- which product types,
- which channels.

Example:

- “For the next 18–24 months, we will:
  - focus on existing SME customers in Country A and B,
  - through digital and assisted digital channels,
  - for working capital and term loans,
  - we will not enter new geographies or launch card products in this horizon.”

This clarity avoids:

- random asks,
- internal confusion,
- scattered investments.

#### 3.4 How We Will Win / Be Valuable

Define **differentiators**:

- Is it speed, personalization, risk insights, integration, pricing, ecosystem, or something else?
- How does this link to **customer jobs and pains** (see earlier phases)?

Example:

- “Our differentiator:
  - deep integration with customers’ accounting and banking data,
  - enabling faster decisions and more tailored offers,
  - with transparent explanations they can trust.”

This should tie to:

- choices in experience design,
- data and analytics investments,
- partnerships and integrations.

### 4. Practical Example – Strategy Outline (Digital SME Lending)

Below is an **example structure** for a product strategy document.

You can write this in `Confluence`, `Notion`, or `Google Docs`.

#### 4.1 Example Structure

- **1. Context & Diagnosis**
  - Market trends: rise of fintechs, expectations of instant credit.
  - Current performance: long decision times, low digital share, high manual overhead.
  - Internal situation: legacy underwriting platform, siloed data.
- **2. Vision**
  - 1–2 sentence statement (as above).
- **3. Strategic Pillars**
  - Pillar 1: “Fast, Transparent Experience”
  - Pillar 2: “Configurable Risk & Compliance Platform”
  - Pillar 3: “Operational Excellence & Automation”
- **4. Where We Will Play**
  - Segments, geos, products, channels (as defined earlier).
- **5. How We Will Win**
  - Differentiators, e.g., data integration + transparency.
- **6. Key Bets & Capabilities**
  - Example bets:
    - New configurable decisioning engine,
    - Unified SME data platform,
    - Self‑service application journey.
- **7. Risks, Constraints & Assumptions**
  - Regulatory change risk,
  - Legacy decommissioning timelines,
  - Hiring and capacity assumptions.

This document should be:

- **reviewed and updated** annually (at least),
- referenced explicitly when crafting roadmaps.

### 5. Common Failure Modes (and Fixes)

#### 5.1 Vision Is Too Vague or Buzzwordy

Examples of weak vision:

- “Be the leading digital bank for SMEs.”
- “Deliver world‑class customer experiences.”

Problems:

- not specific about **who**, **what**, **why**, **how**,
- people can’t use it to guide choices.

Fix:

- apply the simple formula,
- add concrete elements like:
  - “in under X minutes,”
  - “with full transparency,”
  - “configured to Y regulation.”

#### 5.2 Strategy Is Just a Long Backlog

Symptoms:

- your “strategy” is titled:
  - “Product Strategy 2026,”
  - but is actually a list of features.

Fix:

- start with diagnosis and pillars,
- only then map initiatives and features,
- tie each initiative back to:
  - a pillar,
  - a problem,
  - and a measure of success.

#### 5.3 Strategy Document Nobody Reads

Symptoms:

- 80‑page slide deck,
- most teams never open it,
- no decisions reference it.

Fix:

- create a **short core strategy** (e.g., 3–5 pages),
- move deep analysis to:
  - appendices,
  - discovery documents,
  - or links to research.

### 6. How Strategy Connects to Roadmaps

Once strategy is clear, you:

1. Turn pillars into **themes and objectives** (see `05-strategic-themes-goals-and-okrs.md`).
2. From themes and objectives, identify **initiatives and epics**.
3. Prioritize and **sequence** into a roadmap.

Use strategy as a **filter**:

- If a proposed initiative:
  - does not support any pillar,
  - or undermines a key constraint,
- then it should:
  - be challenged,
  - or explicitly added as an exception (with rationale).

---

### 7. Quick Checklist for Vision & Strategy

- [ ] Vision:
  - [ ] states **who** we serve,
  - [ ] states **what** we enable,
  - [ ] states **why** it matters,
  - [ ] is understandable by a new joiner.
- [ ] Strategy:
  - [ ] includes a clear **diagnosis** of the current situation,
  - [ ] defines **3–7 pillars/themes**,
  - [ ] states where we will play and **not** play,
  - [ ] explains **how** we will win or be valuable,
  - [ ] lists key bets & capabilities,
  - [ ] lists major risks/constraints.
- [ ] Document:
  - [ ] is short enough to be read,
  - [ ] is referenced in roadmap and prioritization discussions.
